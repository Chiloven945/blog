Late in the autumn of 1921, seventeen-year-old Ivan received a letter that had been long in coming.

The corners of the envelope were frayed, the postmark faded beyond recognition. Across it ran
brownish-red stains, whether of mud or something else, there was no telling. The letter inside was
folded in three, written in pencil; in places the lead had blurred with water, so that one had to
lean close to the stove fire to make it out.

> "Little brother:
>
> "I am writing to you from a house half-burnt down. There is a hole in the roof, open to the sky.
> When it falls quiet outside, you can hear artillery rumbling far off in the distance, like thunder
> in summer. But it is not summer here.
>
> "Do you remember the summer? That summer by the Volga. You ran barefoot across the riverbank, the
> soles of your feet scorched red against the river stones. I kept shouting after you to come back,
> but you couldn't hear. Uncle was still alive then; when he got drunk, he would lie snoring beneath
> the willows on the shore. We dragged him into the shade so the sun wouldn't bake him. You were so
> small then—you couldn't budge his arms, so you cupped his head in both hands instead. His head was
> heavy. You cradled it, pleading, *'Uncle, Uncle, wake up!'* But he never woke.
>
> "Ivan, do you remember any of this? I write it down because I'm afraid you might forget. If I
> don't make it back, you will be the only one left to remember. And things remembered by two people
> weigh a little more than things kept by one."

Ivan set the sheets down and fed another log into the stove. Perhaps it was the bite of cold air
that made him sniffle.

He remembered. Of course he remembered. Yet he had never known that his brother kept these things so
vivid—vivid enough to put to paper, so vivid that from the pencil strokes one could almost catch the
raw, fishy smell of the Volga.

In the middle of the letter, the pencil had been sharpened; the handwriting grew finer, more
close-set. Ivan imagined his brother sitting in that drafty house by the glow of the hearth, wetting
the pencil lead on his tongue, working out the words one by one.

> "The winter Uncle died, you wept nearly the whole night through. I wouldn't let you cry—not
> because my heart was hard, but because the old Romanov woman lived next door. I was afraid she
> would
> hear; afraid she'd say the Mikhailov boys didn't even know how to mourn with dignity. Later, worn
> out with crying, you burrowed your head beneath my arm and fell asleep. Your hair smelled of stove
> ash.
>
> "The next morning I dragged Uncle outside. He was frozen stiff, one arm raised toward the sky as
> if greeting someone. The snow kept falling, refusing to melt against his face. You stood in the
> doorway, bundled in that sheepskin coat I had cut down for you, the sleeves so long they nearly
> swallowed your hands. You asked me, *'Brother, where has Uncle gone?'* I told you he had gone to
> the
> fields. Then you asked, *'When will he come back?'* I said he would return in the spring.
>
> "Come spring, the river ice broke up with a crack like a rifle butt smashing into timber. I poured
> out all the liquor hidden beneath the floorboards. It soaked into the mud, still smelling of
> Uncle.
> Ivan, I was fourteen then. I figured fourteen was old enough, and that someone old enough ought
> not
> to weep anymore. So I didn't cry. I only smashed the empty bottles on the front stoop, one after
> another, like holiday crackers.
>
> "You came out to watch, clutching a raw potato in your fist. You said, *'Eat, brother. When your
> belly's full, you won't miss Uncle so much.'* Those were my words, stolen by you. At the time I
> thought: when did this boy learn to steal another man's tongue? And then I thought: let him steal
> them. Whatever is mine will belong to you sooner or later anyway."

As Ivan read these lines, the fire dimmed. He rose and stirred the embers. A shower of sparks flew
out, one landing on the margin of the paper and scorching a small amber circle. He pulled the letter
back and blew on it softly.

That scorched mark fell precisely across the words *sooner or later*.

The next section bore a new date. His brother had not written it in one sitting.

> "Last night Petrov asked me why I was always writing. I told him it was to my brother. He said the
> letter would never get out. I told him it didn't matter—writing it was as good as sending it.
> Petrov
> used to be a typesetter in Petersburg; he knows his letters, so he takes words seriously. He said
> if
> a letter isn't posted, it can't be called a letter. I told him he didn't understand. My brother is
> far away, but not so far—only a layer of snow between us. When the snow melts, he will have it.
>
> "Petrov fell silent. He stroked the barrel of his rifle the way he must have stroked his type
> case. Then, out of nowhere, he said, *'I have a brother too. In Kazan. Lost.'* He didn't look at
> anyone when he spoke; he just stared up at that hole in the roof. There were stars in the sky,
> frozen solid, without a flicker.
>
> "Ivan, today I remembered the thrashing I took. Osip's dog at the edge of the village tore your
> trouser leg to shreds, so I took after it with a stick. Osip grabbed me by the scruff, claiming
> you
> had stolen apples from his orchard. I said you hadn't. He said you had. He cuffed me across the
> face. I didn't budge. When his hand came down the second time, I spat right onto his boot. He beat
> me bloody. You stood off in the distance watching me, with a look on your face I will never forget
> as long as I live—not fear, but hunger. That ravenous hunger of wanting to rush in, yet not daring
> to.
>
> "When we got home, you washed my cuts with cold water. As you washed them, you kept saying, *'
Brother, I won't crave apples anymore.'* I told you: crave what you want, your brother will plant
> you an apple tree someday. You said apple trees would never take root along the Volga. Then we'll
> plant something else, I said. Potatoes. Potato blossoms are pretty enough to look at.
>
> "In the end, we never grew those potatoes either. And then I went away. On the day I left, you
> stood at the village boundary, the sleeves of the sheepskin coat hiding your hands. You didn't
> wave.
> I knew you were afraid that if you waved, you'd break down weeping. So was I."

Ivan rested the letter on his knees. Outside the window, someone walked by, their boots crunching
against the frozen earth— *skrip, skrip*. He did not look up.

So his brother had been afraid of crying when he left, too. It turned out that someone who was
supposed to be a grown man at fourteen could still be afraid, even at nineteen.

There was another date, the hand hurried and ragged, blurred with moisture in places—only later did
Ivan realize it was not water, but his brother's hands trembling.

> "Ivan, this is the last page I will write you. Dawn is breaking. They say the enemy will come
> across before first light. I am not afraid. I'm only a little cold. My toes lost all feeling long
> ago, as though someone else's feet were grafted onto my legs.
>
> "I keep this letter tucked against my chest. If it reaches your hands, take it as if I have
> arrived as well.
>
> "There is one thing I never told you. The night before Uncle died, his mind cleared for a short
> spell. He called me over and said, *'Nikolai, the elder looks after the younger. That is the
Mikhailov way.'* I told him I remembered. Then he said he hadn't made much of his life, and warned
> me not to follow in his steps. I said I wouldn't. He seized my hand, his grip very tight, and then
> rasped: *'Vodka.'* I thought he wanted another drink and stood to fetch it. He shook his head and
> said, *'Don't drink. Don't be like me.'*
>
> "I never touched it. Ivan, to this day, not a single drop.
>
> "One more thing: the firewood in the yard needs chopping. If it's too heavy for you, leave it be
> until I return. But remember to sharpen the axe. When you hone it on the stone with water, it
> makes
> that sound—a rasping, squeaking noise, like mice scuttling. You used to be terrified of it as a
> boy;
> you'd clamp your hands over your ears and run away. You aren't afraid of it now, are you? You
> should
> be grown by now.
>
> "I have to stop. Petrov is watching me. He didn't clean his rifle today; he put away his type case
> instead. I don't know how he managed to carry it out here—such a heavy thing. He says when the war
> is over, he's going back to setting type. I asked him what he'd set, and he said newspapers,
> leaflets, poetry. I asked if he had written to his brother. He said no. Said he didn't know what
> to
> write.
>
> "Ivan, I don't know what I've written either. I suppose I only put down whatever came to mind.
> Remember this: the bones of us Mikhailovs don't crack in the cold. You remember that.
>
> "I—"

The letter ended there. No signature, no date. The final word had broken off midway, trailing into a
long pencil mark, like a finger slipping across the paper.

The people at the post office later told Ivan that the letter had reached them through the hands of
a field nurse.

Among a heap of the dead, the nurse had come upon an oilcloth bundle containing a few letters and a
compositor's type case. The type case was stained with dried blood, and the metal letter *М*—the
initial of their family name—had been rubbed smooth and shiny. The nurse sent the letters on to
their addresses, while the type case was left behind in a field hospital, lost to time.

On the day Ivan finished reading the letter, he stood out in the yard, the sheets still clutched in
his hand. The sheepskin coat was too small for him now, the cuffs a good inch too short, leaving his
wrists bare and bitten red by the frost.

In the yard lay a pile of unchopped firewood. An axe leaned against the corner of the wall, its
blade dulled by a thin skin of rust.

He crouched down, picked up a whetstone, scooped a ladle of water from the well, and began to
sharpen the blade. *Skrip, skrip.* The sound rang out through the empty yard, like the scuttling of
mice. He did not cover his ears.

As he split the wood, he thought of that sentence from the letter: *You remember that.* Remember
what? The way potato blossoms looked? Uncle's frozen arm thrust up into the empty air? The shape of
his brother's mouth when he looked back over his shoulder—had he mouthed *Wait for me*, or *Don't
cry*?

And that broken word. The pencil stroke trailing off like a sled rut in the snow that suddenly gave
out.

What had his brother intended to write? *I am gone?* *I am cold?* *I will return?* Or something else
altogether—a word a seventeen-year-old country boy could not read, a word his brother had picked up
from Petrov the typesetter and hadn't yet had the chance to teach him?

He knelt down and gathered the split logs into a pile.

Perhaps his brother had finished writing after all. Not on paper, not at the end of that skittering
pencil mark, but right here—in his hands gripping the axe helve, in the cuffs of the sheepskin coat
pulled tight above his wrists, in this mound of timber waiting out the winter.

He split one log, and then another, keeping at it for a long while. The split wood was stacked
against the wall, catching a dusting of fresh snow.

In the distance, the Volga lay frozen hard. Children were skating across the ice; the wind carried
over their laughter in broken snatches, like signals drifted in from another world.

Ivan straightened his back and gazed down the road leading out of the village. The road was empty.

He folded the letter once more and tucked it against his chest. Beneath the sheepskin, right over
his heart, the paper sat stiff and rough against his ribs.

He was seventeen that year. The road ahead was long, and not a single soul was left behind him. But
against his breast, the letter was warm.

At least the letter was warm.
